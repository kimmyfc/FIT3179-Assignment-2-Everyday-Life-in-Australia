A DATA STORY · EVERYDAY LIFE IN AUSTRALIA · 2026

Ordinary days,
by the numbers.

How do Australians fill their days, where do they go to get out of the house, and do they still feel part of something? This story follows a normal day from the morning routine out into the local community.

Explore the story ↘

INTRODUCTION

What does a normal day actually look like?

Most of life happens between the big moments. It's the school run, the washing up, a walk around the block, story time at the local library and a Saturday shift in the footy club canteen. This page looks at those everyday parts of Australian life, and at whether they still bring people together.

You don't need a background in statistics to follow along. The story moves through five stops, starting with how a single day is spent and ending with how connected people feel. Each stop opens with a question, works through a few charts and wraps up with a short summary.

01 / HOW WE SPEND OUR TIME

Where does
the day go?

Paid work is only one part of the day. Sleep, chores, caring for others and time to unwind all take their share. These are averages across everyone aged 15 and over, including people who didn't do a particular activity that day.

01

Sleep and self-care fill almost half the day

Average time per day · total population · a full ring would be all 24 hours

Read each ring like a clock that starts at 12. Personal care, which includes sleep, fills almost half the dial at 10 hours 43 minutes. Free time comes second at just over five hours, ahead of work and study, because the average includes retirees, students and anyone who had the day off.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1 · See sources

02

Women do twice the housework

Daily minutes by sex · total population

Women spend about twice as long on housework as men, and 26 minutes more each day looking after children. Men get 24 more minutes of free time in return, which adds up to around 146 hours over a year.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1

5 hrsof free time a day, on average

Summary

Almost half of an average day goes to sleep and looking after ourselves. The rest is shared between free time, work or study, and jobs around the house. Paid work looks small here because retirees, students and people on a day off are counted too. The more telling difference is between men and women. Women do about twice the housework and more of the childcare, so they end up with less time to themselves. Women are also more likely to work part-time, which may be part of why more of the load at home falls to them.

Weekly activity ranges from 67.0% in South Australia to 78.3% in the ACT. Most states land within a couple of points of the national figure of 68.8%, which makes the ACT and the NT the real standouts.

04

Walking wins in every state

Participation rate for selected activities · age 15+

Walking is the go-to activity everywhere, with about half of adults heading out for one. Swimming is most popular in the warm NT at 18%, and cycling in the ACT (14%) is twice as common as in NSW.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay activity tables

05

Half of adults move three times a week

Three or more times a week · national benchmark marked by a dark tick

About half of adults get active three or more times a week. The states that do well on weekly activity tend to do well here too, so once people start moving, they often make a habit of it.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay participation overview

7 in 10Australians are active every week

Summary

Most Australians get moving at least once a week, and the most popular way to do it is simply going for a walk. It's free, needs no booking and fits around work and family. The states are fairly close to one another, with one big exception. Canberra leads on every measure and cycles at twice the rate of NSW, helped by a young population and a city built around parks and bike paths. Up north, the NT's warm weather makes it the swimming capital.

03 / SHARED PLACES

Somewhere
to go.

Libraries, community halls and sports centres are where local life happens. They're free or cheap to use, and they give people a reason to leave the house. Libraries also keep national counts, which show how these places fared through the pandemic.

07

Libraries bounced back

National public library measures · each line starts at 100 in 2019–20, so 50 means half that year's level and 150 means half as much again

Visits fell by a third during the lockdown years, then climbed back to 87.6 million, more than before the pandemic. Digital loans grew 62% along the way. Membership kept sliding, though, so plenty of people are using their library without signing up.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, national five-year comparison

08

Story time fills half of all library programs

Program participants, 2023–24 · column width = state's share, segments = audience · TAS, NT and ACT zoomed at right

Half of all library program attendances are early childhood sessions like story time. Teenagers and seniors get only 2–3% each. Victoria runs more programs than NSW, even though it has fewer people.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, programs by target audience

87.6mlibrary visits in 2023–24, more than before COVID

Summary

Shared places follow people, so cities and coastal towns are well served while remote communities have far fewer options. Libraries show how much these spaces are still valued. After emptying out during the lockdowns, they're now busier than before, with families of young children making up a big share of the crowd. Fewer people bother with a library card, but you don't need one to study, use the wifi or come along to story time.

04 / COMMUNITY CONNECTIONS

Taking part.
Giving back.

Going to a show, joining a club and volunteering are all ways of being part of a community. The maps count how many people do each, while the rates show how common it is in each place.

🗺️

Two maps, two ways of joining in

Cultural attendance and formal volunteering in the past year · people age 15+ · same states, different stories

At first glance the two maps look alike. NSW, Victoria and Queensland dominate both, simply because that's where most Australians live, and together they're home to about three in four volunteers.

The rates paint a different picture. Canberra leads for cultural events at 89% but is only average for volunteering, while Tasmania and the NT give the most time per person. The places that love a night out aren't always the ones that roll up their sleeves.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6 · ABS 2021 state boundaries

11

Volunteering is a commitment

Annual hours spent formal volunteering · national estimate

Volunteers are spread fairly evenly across the three bands. Almost a third, about 1.6 million people, give 100 hours or more a year, which is close to two hours every week.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 12

12

Every kind of group involvement fell

National share involved · 2019 to 2025 · "pts" are percentage points, the gap between two percentages

Every kind of group involvement dropped between 2019 and 2025. Volunteering took the biggest hit, falling from 29.5% to 22.6%, so almost one in four volunteers has stepped away.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 1 · See sources

−23%drop in the volunteering rate since 2019

Summary

Most of Australia's volunteers live in NSW, Victoria and Queensland, simply because most people do. Those who volunteer give generously, with about 1.6 million people putting in 100 hours or more a year. What's worrying is the direction things are heading. Volunteering fell from 29.5% to 22.6% between 2019 and 2025, and social and support groups shrank as well. Many clubs closed their doors during COVID, and with living costs rising, people have less spare time to give.

05 / WELLBEING

How are we
really going?

Being busy and involved doesn't always mean feeling connected. These charts look at loneliness, trust, belonging and life satisfaction. The state you picked in section 02 also changes the first two.

13

Nearly one in five often feel lonely

Often feel very lonely · people age 15+

Almost one in five people often feel very lonely. It barely changes from state to state, ranging from 16.3% in NSW to 20.6% in South Australia.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

14

We trust institutions more than each other

Agreement with four statements · Australia

DisagreeNeutralAgree

Most people trust the police (69%) and the health system (61%). We're less sure about each other, with only half agreeing that most people can be trusted.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

15

Younger generations feel they belong less

"Great sense of belonging in Australia" · 2010–12 (hollow) to 2025 (filled)

Belonging dropped in every generation, and the younger the group, the bigger the fall. Australian-born Millennials fell 30 points and Gen X 24, while people born overseas changed far less.

Scanlon Foundation Research Institute, Mapping Social Cohesion 2025, Figure 3

16

Young people haven't bounced back since 2020

Mean score out of 10 · selected age groups · survey years only

People over 65 are the most satisfied in every survey. In 2019, 15–24 year olds were right there with them, but they took the biggest hit in 2020 and haven't fully recovered. The axis starts at 6 so these small shifts are easier to see.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 8

1 in 5Australians often feel very lonely

Summary

Nearly one in five Australians often feel very lonely, and that's true wherever they live. Most of us trust the police and the health system, yet only about half believe most people can be trusted. Younger Australians have had the hardest time. Australian-born Millennials' sense of belonging has dropped 30 points since 2010–12, and 15–24 year olds are still less satisfied with life than before 2020. Expensive housing, insecure work and lockdowns during school and first jobs have all made it harder to settle in and feel at home.

BRINGING IT TOGETHER

Small moments add up
to a community.

Our days are shaped by who we are and where we live. Women still carry more of the work at home, and how active people are depends a little on their state. The places we share are holding up well. Libraries are busier than before the pandemic, and millions still turn up to events and volunteer. But fewer people are joining groups than in 2019, and younger Australians feel less at home than their parents did at the same age. Looking after the everyday places and groups that bring people together may matter more than ever.
