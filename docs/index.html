INTRODUCTION

What does a normal day actually look like?

Most of life isn't made of big moments. It's the morning routine, the dishes, a walk around the block, story time at the local library or a Saturday shift at the footy club canteen. This page uses public data to look at those ordinary parts of Australian life, and at whether they still bring people together.

It's written for everyday Australians, so you don't need any background in statistics. The story moves in five steps, from how we spend a single day out to how connected we feel. Each step asks a question, answers it with a few charts and finishes with a short summary.

01 / HOW WE SPEND OUR TIME

A day is more than
the workday.

Daily averages reveal the quiet weight of personal care, unpaid work and free time. These measures cover the whole population, including people who did not do an activity that day.

01

Where does our time go?

Average minutes per day · total population

Personal care, which includes sleep, takes up 643 minutes a day on average, close to 11 hours. That is more than paid work and unpaid work combined. Free time comes next at around five hours. It looks high, but these averages include retirees, students and anyone who didn't work on the survey day.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1 · See sources & method

02

Who does what?

Daily minutes by sex · total population

Women spend 62 minutes a day on housework compared with 32 for men, and another 26 minutes more on child care. Men end up with about 24 more minutes of free time each day, which adds up to around 146 hours over a year. The survey shows the gap exists but doesn't explain why.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1

Summary

Most of an average day goes on sleep, personal care and unpaid work, and paid work takes up less of it than you might expect. That leaves about five hours of free time, though women get less of it than men because they do more of the housework and child care. The rest of the page looks at how people use that time.

Weekly participation ranges from 67.0% in South Australia to 78.3% in the ACT. Most states sit within a couple of points of the national rate of 68.8%, so the ACT, and to a lesser extent the NT, are the real outliers. The map uses rates rather than counts so small and large states can be compared.

04

Different places, different activities

Participation rate for selected activities · age 15+

Walking is the most common activity in every state, reported by between 48% (NT) and 56% (Tasmania) of adults. Swimming and cycling vary more. Swimming is highest in the NT at 18% and lowest in SA at 12%, and cycling in the ACT (14%) is twice the rate in NSW. People could name more than one activity, so a row won't add up to 100.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay activity tables

05

How regular is participation?

Three or more times a week · national benchmark marked by a dark tick

Around half of adults are active three or more times a week. NSW is lowest at 47.9% and the ACT highest at 59.2%. The states that do well here are mostly the same ones that do well on the weekly map, since people who are active at all often keep it up. Gaps of a point or two are within the survey's margin of error.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay participation overview

Summary

Most Australians are active in some way each week, and walking is the most common activity everywhere. The ACT stands out, but the gaps between the other states are small. Since so much of this activity is informal, local places like parks, paths and community centres matter a lot, which is where the next section picks up.

03 / SHARED PLACES

Somewhere
to go.

Libraries, community halls and sports centres are the everyday infrastructure of local life: free or low-cost places where people meet. Public libraries also publish national counts, so we can see how their use changed through the pandemic.

06

Where are the shared places?

Libraries, community centres and sports centres mapped in OpenStreetMap · one dot per place

There are about 10,000 places on this map, made up of 4,839 sports centres, 3,916 community centres and 1,595 libraries. Almost all of them are along the east and south-east coast or around Perth and Adelaide, with very few in between. They follow the population, which leaves people in remote areas with far less nearby. OpenStreetMap is built by volunteers, so some places will be missing.

07

Libraries bounced back

National public library measures · each indexed to 2019–20 = 100

Visits fell from 84.2 million in 2019–20 to about 56 million during the lockdown years, then climbed to 87.6 million in 2023–24, higher than before the pandemic. Program attendance followed a similar path. Digital loans rose 62% and didn't drop back once libraries reopened. Membership is the odd one out. It fell from 9.3 to 8.1 million even as visits recovered, so plenty of people seem to use libraries without joining.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, national five-year comparison

08

Who goes to library programs?

Program participants, 2023–24 · column width = state's share, segments = audience

Half of the 7.2 million program attendances were early childhood sessions like story time, and children's programs add another 20%. Programs for young adults and seniors are small by comparison, at 2–3% each. Victoria runs more programs than NSW despite having fewer people, and Queensland has the largest adult audience. Hover over the narrow columns on the right to see the smaller states.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, programs by target audience

Summary

Shared places are spread unevenly and mostly follow where people live. Libraries recovered strongly after the pandemic and now get more visits than before, with families of young children making up a big share of program attendance. The fall in membership suggests many people value libraries as somewhere to go, even without joining.

04 / COMMUNITY CONNECTIONS

Taking part.
Giving back.

Cultural venues, groups and volunteering show different ways people take part in public life. Here, counts and rates answer different questions.

09

Who attends cultural events?

Estimated attendees in the past year · people age 15+

About 17.6 million people, or 79% of those aged 15 and over, went to a cultural venue or event in the past year. NSW has the biggest circle with 5.4 million attendees. The rates look a bit different, though. The ACT has the highest rate at 89%, followed by the NT at 83%, while NSW and SA are lowest at around 77%.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6 · ABS boundaries

10

Where do volunteers live?

Formal volunteers in the past year · estimated people

Roughly 5 million Australians volunteered formally, and about three-quarters of them live in NSW, Victoria or Queensland. That mostly reflects population. Looking at rates instead, Tasmania (27.5%) and the NT (26.7%) volunteer the most and Victoria the least (21.8%). The squares are sized by count and only loosely placed by geography.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

11

Volunteering is a commitment

Annual hours spent formal volunteering · national estimate

Volunteers are split fairly evenly across the three bands. 39% gave between 1 and 20 hours over the year, 30% gave 21 to 99, and 31% gave 100 hours or more. That last group is about 1.6 million people giving close to two hours a week or more, and a lot of community work depends on them.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 12

12

Is participation changing?

National share involved · 2019 to 2025

Every type of involvement went down between 2019 and 2025. Formal volunteering fell the furthest, from 29.5% to 22.6%, a drop of almost a quarter. Social group involvement went from 51.1% to 45.3% and support groups from 25.3% to 20.7%. COVID falls between the two surveys, but this data alone can't say how much of the drop it explains.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 1 · Definitions in method

Summary

Most people still take part in community life in some way, whether that's going to cultural events or volunteering, and a large group of volunteers gives 100 hours or more a year. All forms of group involvement are lower than in 2019, though. Fewer people volunteering or in groups means fewer regular chances to meet others, which ties into how connected people feel in the next section.

05 / WELLBEING

Connected, but
how do we feel?

Loneliness, trust, belonging and life satisfaction show how people feel about the communities they live in. The place chosen in Section 02 also applies to the first two charts below.

Feeling lonely

17.9% of people aged 15 and over say they often feel very lonely, which is close to one in five. The rate doesn't change much between states. NSW is lowest at 16.3% and SA highest at 20.6%. You can switch states with the selector in Section 02.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

14

How much do we trust?

Agreement with four statements · Australia

DisagreeNeutralAgree

Most people trust the police (69%) and the health system (61%). Trust in the justice system is lower at 52%. The weakest result is trust in other people, where only half agree that most people can be trusted and about a quarter disagree.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

15

Who feels they belong?

"Great sense of belonging in Australia" · 2010–12 (hollow) to 2025 (filled)

Belonging dropped for every generation of Australian-born adults, and the younger the generation, the bigger the drop. The Silent Generation went from 90% to 85%, Gen X from 77% to 53%, and Millennials from 64% to 34%. Gen Z wasn't surveyed in 2010–12 and is now the lowest at 31%. People born overseas started lower but changed much less, falling by 2 to 8 points.

Scanlon Foundation Research Institute, Mapping Social Cohesion 2025, Figure 3

16

Life satisfaction through the years

Mean score out of 10 · selected age groups · survey years only

People aged 65 and over have the highest life satisfaction in every survey year. 15–24 year olds were level with them in 2019 at 7.7, then fell to 6.9 in 2020, the biggest drop of any group, and had only partly recovered to 7.2 by 2025. The 35–44 group has slowly declined the whole time, from 7.5 to 7.0. The axis starts at 6 so these small changes are easier to see.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 8

Summary

Australians mostly trust institutions like the police and the health system, but trust in other people is lower, and nearly one in five often feel lonely. Younger people have had the biggest falls in belonging and life satisfaction. This lines up with the drop in group involvement in Section 04, although these sources only show the trends happening together, not that one causes the other.

THE STORY SO FAR

Everyday life is made
of unequal time and
shared places.

Women spend more time on housework, weekly activity varies by place, and libraries have refilled since the pandemic. Millions still attend cultural events and volunteer. Yet formal volunteering has fallen since 2019, and younger Australians feel they belong less than their parents did at the same stage. These datasets describe patterns; they do not explain why those patterns occur.

SOURCES & METHOD

Behind the numbers.

This page combines five sources: the ABS Time Use Survey (2020–21), the ABS General Social Survey (2025 with earlier years), AusPlay state and national tables (Australian Sports Commission), the Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24 (ALIA and National and State Libraries Australia) and the Scanlon Foundation Research Institute's Mapping Social Cohesion 2025. Community places come from OpenStreetMap. State maps use simplified 2021 ABS state and territory boundaries in an Albers equal-area projection; the ACT is shown separately on the activity map because it is small at national scale.

Formal volunteering means unpaid work through an organisation in the last 12 months. Cultural attendance means attendance at selected venues or events in the last 12 months. Daily time is an average across the whole population, including people who did not do the activity. Survey estimates have margins of error, and small states and territories are less precise. Survey populations and years differ, so read each chart within its own caption.
