01 / HOW WE SPEND OUR TIME

A day is more than
the workday.

Daily averages reveal the quiet weight of personal care, unpaid work and free time. These measures cover the whole population, including people who did not do an activity that day.

01

Where does our time go?

Average minutes per day · total population

Analysis

Personal care, which includes sleep, takes 643 minutes a day, almost 11 hours. That is more than work and study (252 min) and unpaid work (226 min) put together.

Free time comes second at 304 minutes, about five hours. Because these are averages across everyone, including retirees and people who did not work that day, free time ends up larger than paid work.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1 · See sources & method

02

Who does what?

Daily minutes by sex · total population

Analysis

Women spend about twice as long on housework as men (62 vs 32 min) and 26 more minutes on child care.

Men have 24 more minutes of free time a day. Across a year that adds up to roughly 146 hours, so the unpaid work gap shows up directly as a free-time gap. The chart shows the difference, not its causes.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1

★ Key findings

  • Sleep and personal care fill almost 11 hours a day.
  • Australians average about 5 hours of free time.
  • Women do about twice the housework and have 24 fewer minutes of free time.

✓ Summary

An average Australian day is mostly care, sleep and unpaid work, with paid work smaller than you might expect. Free time is the space where everyday life outside work happens, but it is not shared equally between men and women. The rest of this story follows what people do with that time.

04

Different places, different activities

Participation rate for selected activities · age 15+

Analysis

Walking dominates everywhere, from 48% in the NT to 56% in Tasmania: about one in two adults in every state.

The other activities vary more. Swimming peaks in the warmer NT (18%) and is lowest in SA (12%). Cycling in the ACT (14%) is double NSW (7%). People can report several activities, so rates in a row do not add to 100.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay activity tables

05

How regular is participation?

Three or more times a week · national benchmark marked by a dark tick

Analysis

Half of adults (50.1%) are active three or more times a week. The ACT (59.2%) and NT (54.4%) are clearly above the national tick.

NSW is lowest at 47.9%. States that do well on weekly activity also do well here, which suggests that places where people get out at all also tend to make it a regular habit. These are survey estimates, so gaps of a point or two are within the margin of error.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay participation overview

★ Key findings

  • About 7 in 10 people are active weekly, and half three or more times a week.
  • The ACT leads every measure; NSW, QLD and SA trail slightly.
  • Walking is the everyday activity in every state.

✓ Summary

Most Australians stay active, and the most common way is simply walking. Differences between states are real but modest, about 11 points from top to bottom. Because most activity is informal and close to home, everyday places like parks, paths and community centres matter as much as organised sport.

03 / SHARED PLACES

Somewhere
to go.

Libraries, community halls and sports centres are the everyday infrastructure of local life: free or low-cost places where people meet. Public libraries also publish national counts, so we can see how their use changed through the pandemic.

06

Where are the shared places?

Libraries, community centres and sports centres mapped in OpenStreetMap · one dot per place

1,595libraries

4,839sports centres

Click a type in the legend to highlight it. Hover a dot for its name.

Analysis

The map holds about 10,000 shared places: 4,839 sports centres, 3,916 community centres and 1,595 libraries.

They crowd along the east and south-east coasts and around Perth and Adelaide, then thin out sharply inland. Shared places follow where people live, so remote Australians have far fewer within reach. OpenStreetMap is volunteer-made, so treat this as a pattern rather than a complete list.

07

Libraries bounced back

National public library measures · each indexed to 2019–20 = 100

Analysis

In-person visits dropped from 84.2 million to 56.3 million (about two-thirds) during the closures, then climbed to a new high of 87.6 million in 2023–24. Program attendances fell even further, to 55% of their 2019–20 level, before overtaking it.

Digital loans grew 62% and kept rising after doors reopened. The surprise is membership: registered members fell 13%, from 9.3 to 8.1 million, even as visits recovered. People are using libraries without signing up.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, national five-year comparison

08

Who goes to library programs?

Program participants, 2023–24 · column width = state's share, segments = audience

Analysis

Early-childhood sessions such as story time make up 50% of the 7.2 million program attendances, and children's programs another 20%. Young adults (2%) and seniors (3%) are barely served.

Victoria runs the biggest program load (28%) despite being smaller than NSW (24%). Queensland stands out for adult programs, with more adult participants than any other state. Hover over the thin columns to see the smaller states.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, programs by target audience

★ Key findings

  • About 10,000 shared places are mapped, crowded where people live.
  • Library visits are back above pre-pandemic levels, at 87.6 million.
  • Half of library programs are for early childhood.

✓ Summary

Australia has a dense network of free and low-cost shared places, mostly in cities and along the coast. Libraries show that these places bounced back after the pandemic and are now busier than before, especially for families with young children, even though fewer people hold a membership. Shared places are where the community ties in the next section begin.

04 / COMMUNITY CONNECTIONS

Taking part.
Giving back.

Cultural venues, groups and volunteering show different ways people take part in public life. Here, counts and rates answer different questions.

09

Who attends cultural events?

Estimated attendees in the past year · people age 15+

Analysis

About 17.6 million people (79% of those aged 15+) attended a cultural venue or event in the past year. NSW has the most attendees at 5.4 million.

Rates tell a different story from the circle sizes. The ACT has the highest rate (89%) and the NT is next (83%), while NSW (77%) and SA are lowest. Circle area shows people, not visits.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6 · ABS boundaries

10

Where do volunteers live?

Formal volunteers in the past year · estimated people

Analysis

Of about 5.0 million formal volunteers, NSW (1.55 million), Victoria and Queensland together hold about three-quarters, simply because most people live there.

Per person, the picture flips: Tasmania (27.5%) and the NT (26.7%) have the highest volunteering rates, and Victoria the lowest (21.8%). Square areas show counts; positions are only roughly geographic.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

11

Volunteering is a commitment

Annual hours spent formal volunteering · national estimate

Analysis

The three hour bands are close to even. 39% of volunteers gave 1–20 hours a year, 30% gave 21–99 hours, and 31%, about 1.6 million people, gave 100 hours or more.

So almost a third of volunteers commit the equivalent of two hours a week or more. A lot of community work rests on this committed core.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 12

12

Is participation changing?

National share involved · 2019 to 2025

Analysis

All three kinds of involvement fell between 2019 and 2025. Formal volunteering dropped the most, from 29.5% to 22.6%: about one in four former volunteers has gone.

Social group involvement fell from 51.1% to 45.3%, and support groups from 25.3% to 20.7%. The two surveys sit either side of the pandemic, but this chart cannot show whether it caused the drop.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 1 · Definitions in method

★ Key findings

  • 79% of people attend cultural events each year.
  • About 5 million volunteer; Tasmania and the NT volunteer at the highest rates.
  • Formal volunteering fell from 29.5% to 22.6% since 2019.

✓ Summary

Taking part in community life is still common: most people attend cultural events and millions volunteer, with a committed core giving 100+ hours a year. But every kind of group involvement has fallen since 2019. Fewer volunteers and group members means fewer regular chances to meet people, which leads into how connected Australians feel.

How much do we trust?

Analysis

Trust depends on who is being trusted. Nationally, 69% trust the police and 61% the health system, but only 52% trust the justice system.

The weakest result is about each other: only half (50%) agree most people can be trusted, and a quarter disagree. The bars are centred on the neutral share, so agree and disagree can be compared directly.

15

Who feels they belong?

"Great sense of belonging in Australia" · 2010–12 (hollow) to 2025 (filled)

Analysis

Among Australian-born adults, belonging fell in every generation, and the younger the generation, the bigger the drop. Gen X fell 24 points (77% → 53%) and Millennials 30 points (64% → 34%), while the Silent Generation fell only 5.

Gen Z now has the lowest result of any Australian-born group (31%); they were too young to survey in 2010–12. Overseas-born adults start lower but fell much less, by only 2–8 points.

Scanlon Foundation Research Institute, Mapping Social Cohesion 2025, Figure 3

16

Life satisfaction through the years

Mean score out of 10 · selected age groups · survey years only

Analysis

People aged 65+ are the most satisfied in every survey year (7.7–8.1). Young people aged 15–24 matched them in 2019 (7.7), then fell to 6.9 in 2020, the biggest drop of any group, and are still only at 7.2.

Those aged 35–44 show a slow, steady slide, from 7.5 to 7.0. The axis starts at 6 so these sub-one-point changes are visible, and the lines only join survey years.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 8

★ Key findings

  • Nearly 1 in 5 people often feel very lonely.
  • Only half agree most people can be trusted.
  • Belonging and life satisfaction fell most for younger Australians.

✓ Summary

Australians mostly trust their institutions, but trust in each other is weaker, and loneliness affects almost one in five. The sharpest declines in belonging and life satisfaction are among younger people. That lines up with the fall in group involvement in Section 04, but these datasets show patterns, not causes.

THE STORY SO FAR

Everyday life is made
of unequal time and
shared places.

Women spend more time on housework, weekly activity varies by place, and libraries have refilled since the pandemic. Millions still attend cultural events and volunteer. Yet formal volunteering has fallen since 2019, and younger Australians feel they belong less than their parents did at the same stage. These datasets describe patterns; they do not explain why those patterns occur.

SOURCES & METHOD

Behind the numbers.

This page combines five sources: the ABS Time Use Survey (2020–21), the ABS General Social Survey (2025 with earlier years), AusPlay state and national tables (Australian Sports Commission), the Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24 (ALIA and National and State Libraries Australia) and the Scanlon Foundation Research Institute's Mapping Social Cohesion 2025. Community places come from OpenStreetMap. State maps use simplified 2021 ABS state and territory boundaries in an Albers equal-area projection; the ACT is shown separately on the activity map because it is small at national scale.

Formal volunteering means unpaid work through an organisation in the last 12 months. Cultural attendance means attendance at selected venues or events in the last 12 months. Daily time is an average across the whole population, including people who did not do the activity. Survey estimates have margins of error, and small states and territories are less precise. Survey populations and years differ, so read each chart within its own caption.
