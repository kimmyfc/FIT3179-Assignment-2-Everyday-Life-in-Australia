INTRODUCTION

What does a normal day actually look like?

Most of life isn't made of big moments. It's the morning routine, the dishes, a walk around the block, story time at the local library or a Saturday shift at the footy club canteen. This page uses public data to look at those ordinary parts of Australian life, and at whether they still bring people together.

It's written for everyday Australians, so you don't need any background in statistics. The story moves in five steps, from how we spend a single day out to how connected we feel. Each step asks a question, answers it with a few charts and finishes with a short summary.

01 / HOW WE SPEND OUR TIME

A day is more than
the workday.

Daily averages reveal the quiet weight of personal care, unpaid work and free time. These measures cover the whole population, including people who did not do an activity that day.

01

Sleep and self-care fill almost half the day

Average time per day · total population · a full ring would be all 24 hours

Each ring fills up like a clock as the day is used. Personal care, including sleep, takes almost half the day at 10 hours 43 minutes. Free time comes next at about 5 hours, ahead of work and study, because the average includes retirees, students and people who didn't work that day.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1 · See sources

02

Women do twice the housework

Daily minutes by sex · total population

Women spend about twice as long on housework as men and 26 more minutes on child care. Men end up with 24 more minutes of free time a day, or around 146 hours a year.

ABS, How Australians Use Their Time, Table 1

5 hrsof free time a day, on average

Summary

Once sleep, meals and getting ready are taken out, the rest of the day splits fairly evenly between paid work, unpaid work and free time. Paid work only looks small because the average takes in retirees, students and weekends. What really stands out is who gets the free time. Women do more of the housework and child care, partly because they're more likely to work part-time, and they end up with less time to themselves.

Weekly activity ranges from 67.0% in SA to 78.3% in the ACT. Most states sit within a couple of points of the national 68.8%, so the ACT and NT are the real standouts.

04

Walking wins in every state

Participation rate for selected activities · age 15+

Walking is the most common activity everywhere, done by about half of adults. Swimming peaks in the warm NT (18%), and cycling in the ACT (14%) is double the rate in NSW.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay activity tables

05

Half of adults move three times a week

Three or more times a week · national benchmark marked by a dark tick

About half of adults are active three or more times a week. The states that lead on weekly activity mostly lead here too, so getting active and staying regular seem to go together.

Australian Sports Commission, AusPlay participation overview

7 in 10Australians are active every week

Summary

Staying active is part of normal life for most Australians, and for most of them it simply means going for a walk. It costs nothing and fits around work and family. Canberra stands apart, with a young, well-educated population and a city planned around parks and bike paths. Elsewhere the differences are small, so what matters most is having somewhere close by to get moving.

03 / SHARED PLACES

Somewhere
to go.

Libraries, community halls and sports centres are the everyday infrastructure of local life: free or low-cost places where people meet. Public libraries also publish national counts, so we can see how their use changed through the pandemic.

06

About 10,000 libraries, community and sports centres are mapped, and almost all follow the coastline and capital cities. People in remote areas have far fewer shared places nearby.

07

Libraries bounced back

National public library measures · each indexed to 2019–20 = 100

Visits dropped by a third in the lockdown years, then recovered to 87.6 million, above the 2019–20 level. Digital loans rose 62%, but membership kept falling, so many people now use libraries without joining.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, national five-year comparison

08

Story time fills half of all library programs

Program participants, 2023–24 · column width = state's share, segments = audience

Half of all program attendances are early childhood sessions like story time. Young adults and seniors get only 2–3% each. Victoria runs the most programs, more than NSW despite its smaller population.

ALIA & NSLA, Australian Public Library Statistical Report 2023–24, programs by target audience

87.6mlibrary visits in 2023–24, more than before COVID

Summary

Shared places cluster where people live, so city dwellers have plenty to choose from while remote towns have far fewer. Libraries show how much these spaces matter. After the lockdowns, people came back in bigger numbers than before, many of them parents with young children. Fewer bother with a membership, though. You don't need a card to study, use the wifi or come to story time, and a free, quiet place to spend the day is worth more when money is tight.

04 / COMMUNITY CONNECTIONS

Taking part.
Giving back.

Cultural venues, groups and volunteering show different ways people take part in public life. Here, counts and rates answer different questions.

🗺️

Two maps, two ways of joining in

Cultural attendance and formal volunteering in the past year · people age 15+ · same states, different stories

Both maps are dominated by NSW, Victoria and Queensland, because that's where most people live. Those three states hold about three-quarters of all volunteers.

The rates tell a different story. The ACT leads cultural attendance (89%) but is only average for volunteering, while Tasmania and the NT volunteer the most. Going out to events and giving time don't always happen in the same places.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6 · ABS 2021 state boundaries

11

Volunteering is a commitment

Annual hours spent formal volunteering · national estimate

Volunteers split fairly evenly across the bands. Almost a third, about 1.6 million people, give 100 hours or more a year, close to two hours every week.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 12

12

Every kind of group involvement fell

National share involved · 2019 to 2025

Every kind of involvement fell between 2019 and 2025. Formal volunteering dropped the most, by almost a quarter, from 29.5% to 22.6%.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 1 · See sources

−23%drop in the volunteering rate since 2019

Summary

Australians still show up for each other. Most go to a cultural event each year, and around 1.6 million volunteers give 100 hours or more. Where people live shapes how they take part. Canberra has the national galleries and museums on its doorstep, while smaller communities in Tasmania and the NT rely on volunteers to keep local sport and emergency services going. The worry is the trend. COVID shut many groups down, rising costs have squeezed spare time, and not everyone has come back.

Connected, but

Close to one in five people often feel very lonely. It varies little by state, from 16.3% in NSW to 20.6% in SA.

We trust institutions more than each other

Most people trust the police (69%) and the health system (61%). Trust in other people is weakest, with only half agreeing most people can be trusted.

ABS, General Social Survey 2025, Table 6

15

Younger generations feel they belong less

"Great sense of belonging in Australia" · 2010–12 (hollow) to 2025 (filled)

Belonging fell in every generation, and the younger the generation, the bigger the drop. Millennials fell 30 points and Gen X 24, while overseas-born adults changed much less.

Scanlon Foundation Research Institute, Mapping Social Cohesion 2025, Figure 3

16

Young people haven't bounced back since 2020

Mean score out of 10 · selected age groups · survey years only

People over 65 are the most satisfied in every year. 15–24 year olds matched them in 2019, then had the biggest drop in 2020 and still haven't fully recovered. The axis starts at 6 to show these small changes.

ABS, General Social Survey, Table 8

1 in 5Australians often feel very lonely

Summary

Most Australians trust institutions like the police and the health system, but they're less sure about each other, and nearly one in five often feel very lonely. The sharpest changes are among younger people. Faced with expensive housing and less secure work, they move more often and find it harder to put down roots. Lockdowns also landed on their school years, first jobs and social lives, and their wellbeing hasn't fully recovered.

THE STORY SO FAR

Everyday life is made
of unequal time and
shared places.

Women spend more time on housework, weekly activity varies by place, and libraries have refilled since the pandemic. Millions still attend cultural events and volunteer. Yet formal volunteering has fallen since 2019, and younger Australians feel they belong less than their parents did at the same stage. These datasets describe patterns; they do not explain why those patterns occur.
